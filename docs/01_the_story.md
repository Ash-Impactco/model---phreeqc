# The story of the model, in one page

All numbers are from placeholder soil and rock values, not site results.

**1. Rock arrives.** About 430 t of fine basalt (0 to 4 mm quarry fines from Tecnovia) is trucked about 26 km to Lomba da Maia. Every tonne holds about 8.5 "charge units" (equivalents) of calcium, magnesium, sodium and potassium per kg. If all of it reacted and the carbon stayed put, a tonne could lock away about 320 kg of CO2. That is the ceiling, not what we get.

**2. It gets spread.** Sixteen plots of 80 x 80 m in four blocks down the slope. Within each block one plot gets 0 t/ha (control), one 20, one 40 and one 80, in random order. The rock sits in the top 10 cm of soil because nobody ploughs it in.

**3. It slowly dissolves.** Rain and soil acid attack the surface of each grain, and the grain shrinks from the outside in. Small grains have far more surface per kilo, so they go first: grains finer than 63 micrometres (12% of the mass) give about 70% of what dissolves in the first 30 months, and the 2 to 4 mm grains barely move. In the central case about 7% of the rock's cations are released by month 30 (honest range 3 to 13%). The biggest unknown is how much slower or faster the field is than the lab (the field factor).

**4. It releases calcium and magnesium.** Each released cation has to be balanced by a negative ion. That decides whether carbon is removed.

**5. Some captures CO2.** If the partner is bicarbonate (HCO3-, made from soil CO2), the cation and its bicarbonate wash down through the soil, into streams and eventually to the ocean, where most of it (86%) stays as stored carbon. That is the carbon removal.

**6. Most is used up fighting soil acidity.** This pasture soil is acidic (pH 5.4 placeholder) and keeps being acidified by nitrogen fertiliser and grazing. At pH 5.4 only about 10% of the released cations leave as bicarbonate. The rest neutralise acid: most of them sit on the soil's exchange sites (raising pH and the calcium and magnesium the grass can use), some leave with nitrate, a little is locked into new clay or eaten by grass. This is good for the farm and invisible to the carbon account. As pH rises, the bicarbonate share grows (22% at pH 5.8, 41% at 6.2), so carbon removal speeds up later.

**7. The carbon account.** At 30 months the whole trial has removed about 1.1 t CO2 gross. Trucking, loading and spreading cost 4.3 kg CO2 per tonne of rock the rock's own carbonate another 1.8 kg and its sulfur 0.5 kg: on 358 t of rock that is about 2.4 t CO2 for the trial. Net: about -1.3 t CO2. It turns positive only after several years, and only at 40 and 80 t/ha. So this is a measurement and calibration trial, not a carbon project, and we must not promise credits.

**8. We try to measure it.**
- **Soil cores** (composites of 15 cores per plot, six rounds) show the rock is there and doing something: soil pH, exchangeable Ca + Mg and total Ca + Mg rise with rate, clearly enough with 16 plots if the soil is not too variable.
- **Soil mass balance** (the direct measure of how much rock dissolved) is too noisy to see 7% in 30 months. It stays in as a long-term measurement.
- **Water samplers (lysimeters)** at 30 cm under the 0 and 80 t/ha plots should catch extra alkalinity (about +0.20 meq/L at 80 t/ha, above the lab's 0.05 limit). Streams are far too diluted to see anything.
- **Soil columns in the lab (mesocosms)** at 0 to 160 t/ha for 12 months measure the dissolution and the bicarbonate split directly, and give us the field factor.

**9. What we need next.** December soil screening (pH, buffer capacity, variability between cores), the Tecnovia fines analyses (grain size including the finest tail, chemistry, minerals), and Luis's fertiliser and lime records. Those three pin down the three biggest drivers: field factor, starting pH, fines.
